MRPL blast: police register case against two officials as refinery inquiry begins

One worker died and nine were hurt when a cold separator ruptured at the Jokatte refinery on Wednesday.

A day after the blast and fire at the Coker Hydrotreater Unit of Mangalore Refinery and Petrochemicals Limited (MRPL) near Jokatte, reports say the police have registered a case against two MRPL officials following a complaint. MRPL said a high-pressure cold separator ruptured around midday on Wednesday, 30 September, and a fire followed.

One person died and nine were injured, according to the reports. The man who died was a 30-year-old worker from Mulki. One report says the fire was fully put out by about 2.45 pm. Early counts differed (eight injured in the first reports, nine later), so the paper uses the later figure.

Kambala: racing through the paddy slush

Kambala is the buffalo race of coastal Karnataka. Pairs of buffaloes are driven through a flooded, muddy paddy track by a farmer who runs behind, holding a rope and a plough or a wooden board.

The season usually runs from about November to March, after the harvest. Winners are judged on speed and on how high the mud is thrown, and the events are held across Dakshina Kannada and Udupi, drawing large crowds.

Kambala began as a farmers' celebration of the harvest, and today it is a major sporting tradition with its own rules and a governing body, though it has also drawn debate about the treatment of the animals.

ಮರುದಿನ ಬೆಳಿಗ್ಗೆ ಮಳೆ ನಿಂತಿದ್ದರೂ ಆಕಾಶ ಮುಸುಕಾಗಿಯೇ ಇತ್ತು. ಅಂಗಳದ ಹುಲ್ಲಿನ ಮೇಲೆ ಮಳೆನೀರಿನ ಹನಿಗಳು ಮುತ್ತಿನಂತೆ ನಿಂತಿದ್ದವು. ಹತ್ತು ಗಂಟೆಗೆ ವಕೀಲ ಗೋಪಾಲಕೃಷ್ಣ ಭಟ್ ಸ್ಕೂಟರ್‌ನಲ್ಲಿ ಬಂದರು. ಅರವತ್ತು ವರ್ಷದ ಸಣ್ಣ ಮೈಕಟ್ಟಿನ ಮನುಷ್ಯ, ಕನ್ನಡಕದ ಮೇಲೆ ಕನ್ನಡಕ, ಕಂಕುಳಲ್ಲಿ ಕಪ್ಪು ಚೀಲ, ಕೈಯಲ್ಲಿ ಹಳೆಯ ಕಾಲದ ಕೊಡೆ. ಹಜಾರದ ದೊಡ್ಡ ಮರದ ಪೀಠದ ಮೇಲೆ ಅವರು ಕಾಗದಗಳನ್ನು ಹರಡಿದರು. ಎದುರಿನ ಕುರ್ಚಿಗಳಲ್ಲಿ ಅನನ್ಯಾ, ಸುಮಿತ್ರಮ್ಮ, ಚಿಕ್ಕಪ್ಪ ಮಹಾಬಲ ಶೆಟ್ಟಿ ಕುಳಿತಿದ್ದರು. ಬಾಗಿಲ ಬಳಿ ಕಾವೇರಿ ಅಕ್ಕ ಸೆರಗು ಹಿಡಿದು ನಿಂತಿದ್ದರು. ಮಹಾಬಲ ಚಿಕ್ಕಪ್ಪ ಅಪ್ಪನ ಅಣ್ಣ, ಭಾರವಾದ ಮೈ, ದಪ್ಪ ಮೀಸೆ, ಬೆರಳಲ್ಲಿ ಮೂರು ಚಿನ್ನದ ಉಂಗುರ. ಹಜಾರದ ಗಡಿಯಾರದ ಟಿಕ್ ಟಿಕ್ ಸದ್ದು ಆ ಮೌನದಲ್ಲಿ ಜೋರಾಗಿ ಕೇಳುತ್ತಿತ್ತು.

ವಕೀಲರು ಕನ್ನಡಕ ಸರಿಮಾಡಿಕೊಂಡು ಉಯಿಲನ್ನು ಓದಿದರು. ಮೊದಲ ಸಾಲುಗಳು ಕೃತಜ್ಞತೆ, ಆಶೀರ್ವಾದ, ಕಾವೇರಿ ಅಕ್ಕನಿಗೆ ಒಂದಷ್ಟು ಹಣ, ಸುಮಿತ್ರಮ್ಮನಿಗೆ ಆಜೀವ ವಾಸದ ಹಕ್ಕು. ಮಹಾಬಲ ಚಿಕ್ಕಪ್ಪನಿಗೆ ತೆಂಗಿನ ತೋಟದ ಒಂದು ಭಾಗ. ಅವರು ಅದನ್ನು ಕೇಳಿ ಮೀಸೆ ತಿರುವಿದರು, ಅದು ಸಾಲದು ಎಂಬಂತೆ. ಆಮೇಲೆ ವಕೀಲರು ಧ್ವನಿಯನ್ನು ಸ್ವಲ್ಪ ಗಂಭೀರಗೊಳಿಸಿದರು. “ಸಮುದ್ರ ನಿಲಯ ಮತ್ತು ಅದರ ಸುತ್ತಲಿನ ಮೂರು ಎಕರೆ ಜಮೀನು ನನ್ನ ಮೊಮ್ಮಗಳು ಅನನ್ಯಾಗೆ ಸೇರುತ್ತದೆ.” ಹಜಾರದಲ್ಲಿ ಯಾರೋ ಸಣ್ಣಗೆ ಉಸಿರೆಳೆದರು. ಮಹಾಬಲ ಚಿಕ್ಕಪ್ಪನ ಮುಖ ಕೆಂಪಾಯಿತು, ಅವರ ಕೈ ಕುರ್ಚಿಯ ಹಿಡಿಕೆಯನ್ನು ಬಿಗಿಯಾಗಿ ಹಿಡಿಯಿತು.

“ಆದರೆ,” ಎಂದರು ವಕೀಲರು, “ಒಂದು ಷರತ್ತು ಇದೆ. ಮತ್ತು ಅದು ಉಯಿಲಿನ ಅತ್ಯಂತ ಮುಖ್ಯ ಭಾಗ ಎಂದು ರಾಮಣ್ಣನವರು ಸ್ವತಃ ಹೇಳಿದ್ದರು. ಅನನ್ಯಾ ನಲವತ್ತು ದಿನ ಈ ಮನೆಯಲ್ಲಿ ವಾಸ ಮಾಡಬೇಕು. ಆ ನಲವತ್ತು ದಿನಗಳಲ್ಲಿ ಪ್ರತಿ ಸಂಜೆ ಸೂರ್ಯಾಸ್ತದ ನಂತರ ಮಾಳಿಗೆಯ ದೀಪ ಉರಿಯಬೇಕು. ಒಂದು ದಿನ ತಪ್ಪಿದರೂ ಮನೆ ಮತ್ತು ಜಮೀನು ಊರಿನ ಟ್ರಸ್ಟ್‌ಗೆ ಹೋಗುತ್ತದೆ.”

“ನಲವತ್ತು ದಿನ?” ಅನನ್ಯಾಗೆ ಬೆಂಗಳೂರಿನ ಕೆಲಸ, ಮ್ಯಾನೇಜರ್‌ನ ಮುಖ, ಅಪಾರ್ಟ್‌ಮೆಂಟಿನ ಬಾಡಿಗೆ ಎಲ್ಲವೂ ಒಂದೇ ಬಾರಿ ನೆನಪಾದವು. “ಅಜ್ಜ ಹೀಗೆ ಯಾಕೆ ಬರೆದರು? ಒಂದು ದೀಪಕ್ಕಾಗಿ ಇಷ್ಟೆಲ್ಲಾ?” “ಇದು ಹುಚ್ಚುತನ!” ಎಂದು ಮಹಾಬಲ ಚಿಕ್ಕಪ್ಪ ಗರ್ಜಿಸಿದರು. “ನಲವತ್ತು ದಿನ ಒಬ್ಬ ಹುಡುಗಿ ಕಾಡಿನಂಥ ಈ ಮನೆಯಲ್ಲಿ ಒಬ್ಬಳೇ? ಉಯಿಲನ್ನು ನ್ಯಾಯಾಲಯದಲ್ಲಿ ಪ್ರಶ್ನಿಸುತ್ತೇನೆ!” ಸುಮಿತ್ರಮ್ಮ ಒಂದು ಮಾತೂ ಆಡದೆ ತಲೆ ತಗ್ಗಿಸಿಕೊಂಡೇ ಇದ್ದರು.

ವಕೀಲರು ಹೊರಟ ಮೇಲೆ ಅನನ್ಯಾ ಅಂಗಳದ ತುಳಸಿ ಕಟ್ಟೆಯ ಬಳಿ ಒಬ್ಬಳೇ ನಿಂತಿದ್ದಳು. ಹಿಂದಿನಿಂದ ಭಾರವಾದ ಹೆಜ್ಜೆಗಳು ಕೇಳಿಸಿದವು. ಮಹಾಬಲ ಚಿಕ್ಕಪ್ಪ ಈಗ ಮುಖದ ಮೇಲೆ ಬೇರೆಯೇ ನಗು ಹಚ್ಚಿಕೊಂಡಿದ್ದರು, ವ್ಯಾಪಾರಿಯ ನಗು. “ನೋಡು ಮಗಳೇ, ನನಗೆ ನಿನ್ನ ಮೇಲೆ ಕೋಪವಿಲ್ಲ. ಈ ಮನೆ ಹಳೆಯದು, ಛಾವಣಿ ಸೋರುತ್ತದೆ, ತೋಟಕ್ಕೆ ಆಳು ಸಿಗುವುದಿಲ್ಲ. ನೀನು ನಲವತ್ತು ದಿನ ಈ ಕಾಡಿನಲ್ಲಿ ಹೇಗೆ ಇರ್ತೀಯಾ? ಒಂದು ಕೆಲಸ ಮಾಡು. ಸಹಿ ಹಾಕಿ ಮನೆಯನ್ನು ನನಗೆ ಬಿಟ್ಟುಕೊಡು. ಒಳ್ಳೆಯ ಬೆಲೆ ಕೊಡಿಸುತ್ತೇನೆ. ಹಣ ಬ್ಯಾಂಕಿನಲ್ಲಿರುತ್ತದೆ, ನೀನು ಬೆಂಗಳೂರಿನಲ್ಲಿ ನೆಮ್ಮದಿಯಾಗಿರಬಹುದು.”

“ಅಜ್ಜ ಯಾಕೆ ಹೀಗೆ ಬರೆದರು ಎಂದು ತಿಳಿಯುವವರೆಗೆ ನಾನು ಏನೂ ಸಹಿ ಹಾಕುವುದಿಲ್ಲ ಚಿಕ್ಕಪ್ಪ,” ಎಂದಳು ಅನನ್ಯಾ, ಅವಳೇ ಆಶ್ಚರ್ಯ ಪಡುವಷ್ಟು ಸ್ಥಿರವಾಗಿ. ಚಿಕ್ಕಪ್ಪನ ಕಣ್ಣಲ್ಲಿ ಒಂದು ಕ್ಷಣ ಏನೋ ಮಿಂಚಿ ಮರೆಯಾಯಿತು. “ಹಾಗೇ ಆಗಲಿ,” ಎಂದು ಮೀಸೆ ತಿರುವಿ ಹೊರಟರು. ಹಿಂದೆ ಅಡುಗೆಮನೆಯ ಕಿಟಕಿಯಿಂದ ಕಾವೇರಿ ಅಕ್ಕ ಇದನ್ನೆಲ್ಲ ನೋಡುತ್ತಿದ್ದರು. ಅವರು ತಲೆ ಆಡಿಸಿ ಮೆಲ್ಲಗೆ ಹೇಳಿಕೊಂಡದ್ದು ಅನನ್ಯಾಗೆ ಕೇಳಿಸಲಿಲ್ಲ, ಆದರೆ ತುಟಿಗಳ ಚಲನೆಯಿಂದ ಅವಳಿಗೆ ಅರ್ಥವಾಯಿತು: “ಈ ಹುಡುಗಿ ತನ್ನ ಅಪ್ಪನ ಹಾಗೇ.”

* * *

ಜನ ಹೊರಟ ಮೇಲೆ ವಕೀಲರು ಕೊನೆಯವರಾಗಿ ಹೊರಡುವಾಗ ಅನನ್ಯಾಳನ್ನು ಜಗಲಿಯಲ್ಲಿ ಕರೆದರು. ಅವರು ಕೊಡೆಯನ್ನು ಕೈಯಲ್ಲಿ ತಿರುಗಿಸುತ್ತಾ ಒಂದು ಕ್ಷಣ ಮಾತನಾಡಲಿಲ್ಲ. “ಗೊತ್ತಿಲ್ಲ ಮಗಳೇ ಅವರು ಯಾಕೆ ಹೀಗೆ ಬರೆದರು ಎಂದು,” ಎಂದರು ಕೊನೆಗೆ. “ಆದರೆ ಸಾಯುವ ಮೊದಲಿನ ವಾರ ಅವರು ನನ್ನನ್ನು ಕರೆಸಿದ್ದರು. ಆರೋಗ್ಯ ತುಂಬ ಕೆಟ್ಟಿತ್ತು, ಆದರೂ ಎದ್ದು ಕುಳಿತು ಈ ಉಯಿಲನ್ನು ಮೂರು ಬಾರಿ ತಿದ್ದಿಸಿದರು. ಮತ್ತು ಪದೇ ಪದೇ ಒಂದೇ ಮಾತು ಹೇಳುತ್ತಿದ್ದರು, ‘ಸಾಗರಿ ಮತ್ತೆ ಬರ್ತಾಳೆ. ದೀಪ ಆರಬಾರದು.’”

ಅನನ್ಯಾ ಮೊದಲ ಬಾರಿ ಆ ಹೆಸರನ್ನು ಕೇಳಿದಂತೆ ಇರಲಿಲ್ಲ. ಸಾಗರಿ ಅಜ್ಜನ ಹಳೆಯ ದೋಣಿಯ ಹೆಸರು. ಅವಳು ಹುಟ್ಟುವ ಮೊದಲಿನಿಂದಲೂ ಆ ಹೆಸರು ಮನೆಯ ಮಾತುಗಳಲ್ಲಿತ್ತು. ಇಪ್ಪತ್ತೈದು ವರ್ಷದ ಹಿಂದೆ, ಅವಳಿಗೆ ಎರಡು ವರ್ಷ ಆಗಿದ್ದಾಗ, ಒಂದು ಚಂಡಮಾರುತದ ರಾತ್ರಿ ಅವಳ ತಂದೆ ರಘುನಾಥ ಆ ದೋಣಿಯಲ್ಲಿ ಹೋದರು, ಮತ್ತೆ ಹಿಂದಿರುಗಲಿಲ್ಲ. ಅವರ ದೇಹವೂ ಸಿಗಲಿಲ್ಲ, ದೋಣಿಯ ಒಂದು ಹಲಗೆಯೂ ಸಿಗಲಿಲ್ಲ. ಅಮ್ಮ ಕೆಲವು ವರ್ಷಕ್ಕೆ ಅವಳನ್ನು ಕರೆದುಕೊಂಡು ಬೆಂಗಳೂರಿಗೆ ಹೋದರು. ಆ ಹೆಸರನ್ನು ಅವರು ಮತ್ತೆ ಎಂದೂ ಉಚ್ಚರಿಸಲಿಲ್ಲ.

ಅವಳು ಹಿಂದಿರುಗಿ ಹಜಾರದೊಳಗೆ ನೋಡಿದಾಗ ಸುಮಿತ್ರಮ್ಮ ಒಬ್ಬರೇ ಕುಳಿತಿದ್ದರು. ಅವರ ಕೈಯಲ್ಲಿದ್ದ ಚಹಾ ಲೋಟ ತುಸು ನಡುಗುತ್ತಿತ್ತು. ಆ ಮುಖದ ಮೇಲೆ ಅನನ್ಯಾ ಏನನ್ನೋ ಓದಿದಳು, ಅದು ದುಃಖವಲ್ಲ, ಕೋಪವಲ್ಲ, ಭಯ. ಇಷ್ಟು ವರ್ಷದ ನಂತರ ಒಂದು ಹೆಸರು ಕೇಳಿ ಹುಟ್ಟಿದ ಭಯ. ಅಂದೇ ಅವಳು ತೀರ್ಮಾನಿಸಿದಳು: ಬೆಂಗಳೂರಿಗೆ ಫೋನ್ ಮಾಡಿ ರಜೆ ಹೆಚ್ಚಿಸಿಕೊಳ್ಳುತ್ತೇನೆ. ನಲವತ್ತು ದಿನ ಈ ಮನೆಯಲ್ಲೇ ಇರುತ್ತೇನೆ. ಈ ಮನೆ ಏನನ್ನು ಮುಚ್ಚಿಟ್ಟಿದೆಯೋ, ಅದನ್ನು ಕಂಡುಹಿಡಿಯುತ್ತೇನೆ.

ಮುಂದಿನ ಸಂಚಿಕೆ ನಾಳೆ ಇದೇ ಪುಟದಲ್ಲಿ.

ಕರಾವಳಿಯ ಕಡಲಕೆರೆ ಎಂಬ ಕಾಲ್ಪನಿಕ ಊರಿನ ಕಥೆ. ಇದರಲ್ಲಿರುವ ಎಲ್ಲ ಪಾತ್ರಗಳು ಮತ್ತು ಘಟನೆಗಳು ಕಾಲ್ಪನಿಕ.

ಹಿಂದಿನ ಸಂಚಿಕೆಗಳು (1)
ಸಂಚಿಕೆ ೧: ಮಳೆಯ ರಾತ್ರಿ

ಬೆಂಗಳೂರಿನಿಂದ ಹೊರಟ ರಾತ್ರಿ ಬಸ್ಸು ಕಡಲಕೆರೆಯ ನಿಲ್ದಾಣದಲ್ಲಿ ಕೊನೆಗೂ ನಿಂತಾಗ ಸಂಜೆ ಏಳು ಗಂಟೆ ಕಳೆದಿತ್ತು. ಮೂರು ದಿನದಿಂದ ಬಿಡದೆ ಸುರಿಯುತ್ತಿದ್ದ ಮಳೆ ಇನ್ನೂ ನಿಂತಿರಲಿಲ್ಲ. ರಸ್ತೆ ಬದಿಯ ಅಂಗಡಿಗಳ ಮುಂದೆ ಜನ ಕೊಡೆ ಹಿಡಿದು ನಿಂತಿದ್ದರು; ಚಹಾದ ಅಂಗಡಿಯ ಒಲೆಯಿಂದ ಹೊಗೆ ಮಳೆಯೊಂದಿಗೆ ಬೆರೆತು ಮೇಲೇರುತ್ತಿತ್ತು. ಅನನ್ಯಾ ಬಸ್ಸಿನಿಂದ ಇಳಿದು ತನ್ನ ದೊಡ್ಡ ಸೂಟ್‌ಕೇಸನ್ನು ನೆಲಕ್ಕೆ ಇಟ್ಟು ಕೊಡೆ ಬಿಚ್ಚಿದಳು. ಆದರೆ ಕಡಲ ಕಡೆಯಿಂದ ಬೀಸಿದ ಗಾಳಿ ಕೊಡೆಯನ್ನು ಅವಳಿಗಿಂತ ಬಲವಾಗಿ ಹಿಡಿದೆಳೆಯಿತು, ಅದರ ಒಂದು ಕಡ್ಡಿ ಬಾಗಿಯೇ ಹೋಯಿತು. ಅವಳು ನಗುವುದೋ ಅಳುವುದೋ ತಿಳಿಯದೆ ನಿಂತಳು. ಬೆಂಗಳೂರಿನ ಕಚೇರಿಯಲ್ಲಿ ಮ್ಯಾನೇಜರ್ ಹತ್ತು ದಿನದ ರಜೆ ಕೊಡಲು ಎಷ್ಟು ಹಿಂದೇಟು ಹಾಕಿದರು ಎಂಬುದು ಅವಳಿಗೆ ನೆನಪಾಯಿತು. “ಅಜ್ಜನ ಉಯಿಲಿಗೆ ನೀವೇ ಬರಬೇಕೇ? ಆನ್‌ಲೈನ್‌ನಲ್ಲಿ ಆಗಲ್ವಾ?” ಎಂದಿದ್ದರು. ಅಂಥ ಮಾತುಗಳಿಗೆ ಅವಳ ಬಳಿ ಉತ್ತರವೇ ಇರಲಿಲ್ಲ.

“ಅನನ್ಯಮ್ಮಾ! ಇಲ್ಲಿ, ಇಲ್ಲಿ!” ದೂರದಿಂದ ಒಂದು ಕರೆ ಬಂತು. ಹಳದಿ ರೇನ್‌ಕೋಟ್ ಹಾಕಿಕೊಂಡ ವೃದ್ಧೆಯೊಬ್ಬರು ದೊಡ್ಡ ಕಪ್ಪು ಕೊಡೆ ಹಿಡಿದು ಕೈ ಬೀಸುತ್ತಿದ್ದರು. ಕಾವೇರಿ ಅಕ್ಕ. ಇಪ್ಪತ್ತು ವರ್ಷದಿಂದ ಅಜ್ಜನ ಮನೆಯಲ್ಲಿ ಅಡುಗೆ, ಮನೆಕೆಲಸ ಎಲ್ಲವನ್ನೂ ನೋಡಿಕೊಂಡವರು. ಅವಳನ್ನು ಬಾಲ್ಯದಲ್ಲಿ ಎತ್ತಿ ಆಡಿಸಿದ ಕೈಗಳು ಅವು. “ಎಷ್ಟು ಬೆಳೆದುಬಿಟ್ಟಿದ್ದೀಯಾ ಮಗಳೇ,” ಎನ್ನುತ್ತಾ ಅವಳ ಕೆನ್ನೆ ಮುಟ್ಟಿದರು, ಕಣ್ಣು ತುಂಬಿ ಬಂತು. “ಸೊರಗಿ ಹೋಗಿದ್ದೀಯಲ್ಲ, ಅಲ್ಲಿ ಊಟ ಸರಿಯಾಗಿ ಮಾಡ್ತೀಯಾ ಇಲ್ವಾ? ಬಾ, ಮೊದಲು ಒಳಗೆ ಬಾ. ಕಾಫಿ ಇಟ್ಟಿದ್ದೀನಿ.” ಇಬ್ಬರೂ ಒಂದೇ ಕೊಡೆಯಡಿ ಇಕ್ಕಟ್ಟಾಗಿ ನಡೆದರು. ಅಡಕೆ ತೋಟದ ನಡುವಿನ ಕಾಲುದಾರಿ ಕೆಸರಾಗಿತ್ತು. ಒದ್ದೆ ಮಣ್ಣಿನ ವಾಸನೆ, ಕಡಲ ಉಪ್ಪುಗಾಳಿ, ದೂರದಲ್ಲಿ ಅಲೆಗಳ ಗರ್ಜನೆ ಎಲ್ಲವೂ ಒಟ್ಟಿಗೆ ಅವಳನ್ನು ಸುತ್ತುವರಿದವು. ಎಂಟು ವರ್ಷದ ಹಿಂದೆ ಈ ದಾರಿಯಲ್ಲೇ ಅವಳು ಬರಿಗಾಲಲ್ಲಿ ಓಡಾಡಿದ್ದಳು.

ಹನ್ನೆರಡು ದಿನದ ಹಿಂದೆ ಅಜ್ಜ ರಾಮಣ್ಣ ಶೆಟ್ಟಿ ತೀರಿಕೊಂಡಾಗ ಅನನ್ಯಾ ವಿದೇಶದ ಯಾವುದೋ ಸಭೆಯ ನಡುವೆ ಇದ್ದಳು. ಸುದ್ದಿ ಬಂದಾಗ ಬೇಗ ಹೊರಟರೂ ಅಂತ್ಯಕ್ರಿಯೆ ಮುಗಿದುಹೋಗಿತ್ತು. ಆ ನೋವು ಇನ್ನೂ ಅವಳ ಎದೆಯಲ್ಲಿ ಕಲ್ಲಿನಂತೆ ಕುಳಿತಿತ್ತು. ಕೊನೆಯ ಬಾರಿ ಅವಳು ಕಡಲಕೆರೆಗೆ ಬಂದಿದ್ದು ಎಂಟು ವರ್ಷದ ಹಿಂದೆ. ಆಗ ಅಜ್ಜ ಮಾಳಿಗೆಯ ಮೇಲೆ ಅವಳ ಕೈ ಹಿಡಿದು ಹೇಳಿದ್ದರು, “ಮನೆಯ ದೀಪ ಎಂದೂ ಆರಬಾರದು ಮಗಳೇ. ಆರಿದ ದಿನ ಈ ಮನೆಗೆ ಅರ್ಥವೇ ಇರುವುದಿಲ್ಲ.” ಅವಳಿಗೆ ಆಗ ಅದು ಹಳೆಯ ಕಾಲದ ಮುದುಕರ ಮಾತಿನಂತೆ ಕೇಳಿಸಿತ್ತು. ಈಗ ಅದು ಉಸಿರು ಬಿಗಿಹಿಡಿಯುವಷ್ಟು ಭಾರವಾಗಿ ನೆನಪಾಯಿತು.

PreviouslyIra Rao is at her desk in a Mangaluru newsroom when a retired clerk, soaked by the monsoon, hands her a ledger and a warning. She takes it home.

Ira’s flat was on the first floor of an old house in Kodialbail, above a tailor’s shop whose sewing machines went quiet at eight every night and began again, with a sound like a very small and very determined train, at half past eight the next morning. It had two rooms and a balcony that held a bucket and a bicycle. The landlady, Mrs D’Souza, lived downstairs and had views on everything from Ira’s hours to the state of the nation, but she also left a steel tiffin of rice and fish curry on the stairs on Wednesdays without ever mentioning it, which Ira understood to be love.

She ate the curry standing at the counter, because she was too restless to sit. Then she made a glass of ginger tea, spread an old bedsheet over the table for no reason she could name except that it seemed wrong to open the thing on a bare surface, and finally, with the rain drumming on the tin roof of the verandah below, she opened the ledger.

It was a plain accounts book, ruled in faded red, filled with entries in a small, upright, extraordinarily careful hand: dates, boat numbers, quantities of diesel, quantities of ice, the cost of nets mended and engines repaired. Page after page of it, thirty-one years’ worth of ordinary arithmetic, in the blue ink of someone who believed that a number written clearly could not be argued with. Ira turned the pages for a quarter of an hour and felt her eyes begin to slide. Anyone who has ever tried to read an accounts book for pleasure will understand. She almost closed it. She actually had her hand on the cover when she noticed that the entries on the right-hand pages had begun to change.

It was subtle. The handwriting was the same, but in the last three years of entries the columns had grown a seventh, headed in neat capitals ICE AND DIESEL, SEA CREST MARINE. The payments in column seven were not like the others. They were regular, they were large, and they were made to the same company, month after month, for boat after boat after boat. She counted. In March alone, the cooperative had paid Sea Crest Marine for ice and diesel for ninety-four boats. In April it was ninety-eight. By the time the pages reached the third winter it was a hundred and fourteen.

Ira, who was not good at sums but was very good at being suspicious of them, went and found a pencil and a page of rough paper and began to work. The Sasi Fishermen’s Cooperative, she knew from her own diesel-queue story, represented about sixty working boats. She had interviewed the secretary. She had counted the boats in the harbour with her own eyes, standing on the jetty in a drizzle, with a photographer who had kept complaining about his lens. Sixty boats. Not a hundred and fourteen. She added the page twice, and the second time more slowly, with her finger running down the column, and the total did not change. It was not a clerical slip. It was a number that someone had meant.

* * *

She might have talked herself out of it even then, because she was a reasonable person and reasonable people look for reasonable explanations. Perhaps the cooperative supplied boats from other harbours. Perhaps ice and diesel were bought in bulk for the whole coast. But at the bottom of that last page, in the thin margin beside the final total, there was a line in pencil, so faint that she had to tilt the lamp to catch it. It was the same small, upright hand, but pressed so lightly it looked like a thing written by someone who wanted it to be found and did not want it to be seen.

Boat 114 does not exist.

Ira sat very still. Outside, a scooter went through the puddle at the corner with a long sigh. Downstairs the tailor’s cat knocked something off a shelf and was shouted at in Konkani. The lamp hummed. She thought of the old man on the newsroom floor with his broken umbrella, saying read it where the sea cannot hear, and she thought, for the first time, that perhaps he had not been strange at all. Perhaps he had simply been afraid in the ordinary, well-informed way of someone who knows exactly how much a number is worth.

She took out her phone and photographed the page, then the one before it, then every page with a seventh column, working carefully, because the light was bad and her hands were not entirely steady. When she had finished it was after midnight and the rain had thinned to a whisper. She wrote three words at the top of a clean sheet, BOAT 114, and underlined them twice. Then she sat and watched the sheet as though it might reply.

At a quarter to one, she called her mother back. It was a ridiculous time, and her mother answered on the first ring, alarmed, and Ira said, “Amma, I’m fine. I only wanted to say, tell me about the chartered accountant,” and heard her mother go silent with astonishment, and then begin, slowly and with enormous care, to describe him, as though Ira were a deer that might bolt. It was, Ira found, a surprisingly comforting thing to listen to. A suitable boy, a flat in Udupi, a nice nature. She let her mother’s voice run on, warm and ordinary, and looked at the ledger on the table, which was neither.

Episode 1: The Man with the Wet Umbrella

The monsoon had been sulking over Mangaluru for nine days, and by six o’clock that Wednesday evening the newsroom of The Coastal Courier smelled of wet paper, stale tea and the particular damp of a building that has never once been properly dry. Ira Rao sat at the desk nearest the window, typing up a story about a pothole on the road to Kodialbail that had swallowed an auto-rickshaw whole, and she was thinking, with some bitterness, that this was probably the most exciting thing that would happen to her all week. She was twenty-six. She had been at the paper for eleven months. In that time she had covered two ribbon-cuttings, a goat that had walked into a bank, and the long, slow, unresolved quarrel over who owned the parking space outside the Hampankatta fruit market. Her editor said she had a nose for a story. Ira privately felt the nose had so far found only the goat.

The door at the far end opened, and a man came in with an umbrella that had clearly lost its argument with the wind. Two of its ribs poked out like the bones of a small, tired bird. Water ran off his shirt and trousers and made a little lake on the floor around his chappals, and the peon, Sheshappa, who had been about to say something about wet floors, took one look at the man’s face and said nothing at all. He was perhaps sixty-eight, thin, with white hair combed flat in the careful way of a man who had once worked in an office and still dressed for it. He stood at the doorway and looked around the room slowly, the way a person looks around a bus station, checking every face. Then he looked at Ira, and his shoulders came down a fraction, as though he had found what he had come for.

“Miss Rao?” he said. “Ira Rao? You wrote about the diesel queue at Malpe.”

“Three months ago,” said Ira. She was surprised to find that she had stood up. “Nobody read it.”

“I read it. I cut it out and kept it.” He crossed the room, leaving wet footprints, and set a plastic bag on her desk. Inside, wrapped twice in newspaper, was a ledger with a cloth cover gone soft at the corners, the sort of bound accounts book that nobody had bought in a stationery shop for twenty years. “My name is Anthony Pinto. I was a clerk at the Sasi Fishermen’s Cooperative for thirty-one years. I retired two winters ago. You will not find my name in anything, Miss Rao, because clerks’ names are never in anything. That is the whole point of a clerk.”

Ira put a hand on the plastic bag but did not open it. “Mr Pinto, what is this?”

“It is the truth,” said Mr Pinto, “written in blue ink, in my own hand, over three years. It is not a pleasant truth and I have not been brave about it. I should have brought it a year ago. I have been sitting in my house for a year listening to the rain and telling myself that someone else would notice.” He swallowed. His throat moved like a boy’s. “Do not read it here. Do not read it in this building. Take it home, and read it somewhere the sea cannot hear.”

There was a short silence in which the ceiling fan ticked and somewhere in the next room a telephone rang and was ignored. Ira said, carefully, “That is a strange thing to say.”

“I know.” He tried to smile and did not quite manage it. “I am a strange old man. My sister says so. Good evening, Miss Rao. Thank you for writing about the queue.” He took up his broken umbrella, nodded gravely to Sheshappa, who nodded back as if they were two diplomats, and went out through the same door, leaving the lake behind him. Through the window, Ira watched him go down the stairs and out into the street, where the rain closed over him like water over a stone.

* * *

She did not read it in the newsroom. She did not, in fact, so much as open the plastic bag until she had finished the pothole story, filed it, cleared her desk, said goodnight to the sub-editors, and walked the long way round to the bus stop with the bag held against her ribs under her raincoat as though it were an injured animal. The bus to Kodialbail was full of wet umbrellas and the smell of frying onions from the roadside stalls, and at the Hampankatta signal it stopped for so long that the conductor got down and bought a packet of banana chips. Ira stood holding the rail and looked at the faces around her: a schoolgirl asleep on her mother’s shoulder, two men arguing in Tulu about a cricket match, a woman with a basket of jasmine garlands wrapped in banana leaf, the scent of them rising warm and sweet through the diesel and rain. It struck her that every one of these people had a ledger of some kind at home, in a drawer, in a trunk, in the back of their head, and that most of them would never hand it to a stranger on a wet Wednesday.

Her phone buzzed. It was her mother, which meant it would be about marriage. Ira let it ring out. She would call back after dinner and listen to a description of a very suitable boy, a chartered accountant from Udupi with a flat and a nice nature, and she would say, as she always did, that she was busy, and her mother would say that nobody was too busy for happiness. It was a conversation she could have recited in her sleep. For the first time in months she found that she did not mind having a reason to postpone it.

The bus lurched forward at last. Ira closed her eyes and held the bag a little tighter, and tried, without much success, to remember what kind of man puts a newspaper cutting about a diesel queue in his pocket and keeps it for three months.

Herb of the day

Curry leaf

ಕರಿಬೇವುMurraya koenigii
Kitchen gardenEvergreen

The kitchen tree behind every tempering. It loves warmth, so it settles in easily on the coast and needs no protection from cold.

Light
Full sun.
Water
Deeply but not often. Let the top 3 to 5 cm dry between waterings to avoid root rot.
Soil
Well-drained soil that is rich in compost.
Feed
Compost or organic manure every two to three months. Nitrogen-rich feed gives lush leaves.
Harvest
Pick leaves regularly from the tips so it keeps branching.
Flower of the day

Aboli

ಕನಕಾಂಬರCrossandra infundibuliformis
Udupi favouriteFlowers all year

Firecracker flower in English, Kanakambara in Kannada and Aboli in Konkani and Marathi. Its orange and coral blooms are strung into garlands across the coast.

Light
Bright morning sun, with cover from harsh afternoon sun.
Water
Thoroughly, whenever the top few inches of soil are dry. Under-watering harms it.
Soil
Fertile, well-drained red loam, pH 6 to 7.5.
Climate
Warm and humid, 20 to 30 degrees C. It suits Mangaluru.
Feed
Liquid fertiliser every two weeks in the growing season.
Indoor and garden plant

Monstera

Monstera deliciosa
IndoorLoves humidity

The split-leaf climber that enjoys Mangaluru's humid air. It grows impressively through the monsoon months.

Light
Bright spot, away from direct afternoon sun.
Water
Moderate. Water only when the top of the soil starts to dry.
Soil
Mix in coarse sand or perlite for drainage.
Air
Give it airflow and watch for fungal spots in wet weather.

This month in the garden

  • Check every pot has drainage holes, and clear the saucers after rain.
  • Mulch bare soil to stop splash and keep roots steady.
  • Give ferns and balcony plants airflow to avoid fungus.
  • Sow spinach, amaranth and fenugreek, which enjoy the humidity.
  • Shield young plants from strong wind and heavy downpours.
  • Mix coarse sand into laterite soil so roots do not sit wet.
